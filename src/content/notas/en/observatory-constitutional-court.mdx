## What the observatory is

The Judicial Rulings Observatory is a project to explore how Guatemala's justice system behaves over time. The idea is simple: instead of looking at one famous case, look at every case and see what patterns appear.

So far it covers only the rulings of the Constitutional Court (Corte de Constitucionalidad, or CC), the court with the final word on the Constitution. The websites of other institutions in the justice system were also checked, but they do not publish the information needed. The next step is to request it directly, through public information requests, to add more courts.

## What was measured

In Guatemala, an *amparo* is a lawsuit that asks a court to protect a constitutional right. When a judge decides an amparo, whoever disagrees can appeal to the CC. The CC then does one of two things:

- it **changes** the decision: it reverses it, annuls it, or sides with the person who appealed, even if only in part;
- it **upholds** the decision: it leaves it as it was.

The **reversal rate** is the share of cases in which the CC changed the decision. If it decided 100 appeals and changed 24, the rate is 24%.

When the CC upholds the decision and only changes something small, such as the amount of a fine or a deadline, the case was counted as upheld. When it sides with the person who appealed, even in part, it was counted as changed.

## How it was done

1. **Download.** Only the institutions' public websites were used. From the [CC's case-law portal](https://jurisprudencia.cc.gob.gt/ptmp/), every amparo appeal ruling published up to October 9, 2026 was downloaded, a total of **48,656**. No password was used and no access control was bypassed, and the downloads were made slowly so as not to overload the site. Each document keeps its web address and a "digital fingerprint" that shows whether it has changed.
2. **Read.** Artificial intelligence tools (Claude Code) were used to explore the portal's pages and to write the programs that download the documents, extract their text, and find the final part of each ruling, where the CC states what it decided ("POR TANTO… resuelve").
3. **Classify.** Whether each case changes or upholds the decision is not decided by artificial intelligence but by a program with fixed rules that looks for phrases such as "se revoca" (it is reversed) or "se confirma" (it is upheld). The same document always gives the same result, and anyone can check why.
4. **Review.** A person read 388 randomly chosen rulings and compared their reading with the program's. They matched in 387. The one error was used to fix a rule.

Each figure comes with the number of cases behind it and its margin of error, shown in the charts as the lines on either side of each dot. When the margins of two figures overlap, the difference between them may be chance.

**What is not visible.** Only what the CC published: if it stopped publishing certain kinds of cases, it would not show here. 826 documents are left out of the count because they do not rule on a judge's decision (clarifications, for example) or because their decision could not be read clearly, and 69 could not be downloaded. 2025 and 2026 may be incomplete, because the CC takes time to publish. Also:

- Cases are not yet broken down by subject or by who appeals. If the cases that reach the CC change, the figure changes even if the Court decides the same way.
- The CC decides as a body and does not publish how each justice voted, so no figure belongs to any one person.
- The rulings of the Supreme Court of Justice and the Courts of Appeals cannot be consulted without a lawyer's credentials.

## Pattern 1: by term

The CC works in five-year terms (*magistraturas*), each with the same group of justices. From term I to term VII, the CC changed between 20.4% and 24.3% of the decisions appealed to it: roughly one in five. In term VIII (2021-2026) it rose to 29.9%, almost three in ten. Its margin of error does not overlap with any other term's.

## Pattern 2: by the year the case was filed

Another way to sort the cases is by the year the case was filed, regardless of which term decided it. Cases filed from 2020 to 2023 were changed 31.0% of the time; those from earlier years, between 20.8% and 23.8%. It is the same jump seen from another angle.

## Pattern 3: how each term begins

How is term IX doing? The only way to tell is to compare it with the same stretch of the others: April 14 to July 31 of their first year. In those three and a half months, terms IV through IX all changed between 15.2% and 21.4% of decisions. Term IX, at 21.1%, begins like the ones before it.
